Turning a gift card into cash, without leaving users guessing

A self-serve flow for selling Amazon and Flipkart gift cards, designed to make the amount, verification and payout clear.

Role
End-to-end product design
Collaborators
Product manager and engineering
Scope
Flow design, prototyping and developer handoff
Tools
Claude, Figma Make and Yaper Design System

At a glance

Yaper’s cashback orders relied on external cardholders for fulfilment. Product and support inputs pointed to operational friction in that model, including unpredictable availability, processing fees and delays.

The team explored buying discounted gift cards directly from consumers and using those balances to fulfil cashback orders. I worked with product and engineering on the end-to-end design for that flow, from card entry through verification and UPI payout.

Because a gift-card number and PIN can unlock real value, the experience needed to explain what a user would receive and what would happen after they submitted their details. The proposed change was projected to increase commission per order by 30% or more by reducing the cardholder split and processing fees. The feature had not launched, so this was a projection, not a measured outcome.

01

Why we explored a new fulfilment model

Yaper depended on external cardholders to fulfil cashback orders. That created operational challenges: cardholders were not always available when needed, processing fees reduced the commission, and delays contributed to fulfilment backlogs.

The proposed model was to buy discounted Amazon and Flipkart gift cards directly from consumers, then use those balances to fulfil cashback orders.

The business case projected a 30%+ lift in commission per order by reducing the cardholder split and processing fees. Since the feature was handed off but not live, we had no post-launch result to report.

02

The user problem was trust

Selling a gift card is different from entering an ordinary payment detail. A user shares a card number and PIN that may hold real value. They need to understand the transaction before they hand over those details.

I used product and support inputs to shape the flow around four questions:

  • 1How much will I receive? Show the expected payout amount, not only a discount rate.
  • 2What’s happening to my card? Explain verification and show the current status.
  • 3What if the card is rejected? Give a clear reason and explain what the user can do next.
  • 4When will I get my money? Separate verification from payout and set a clear expectation: UPI payout is instant after the card is verified.
03

I worked out the flow before polishing the screens

I started with the transaction logic, explored it as a clickable flow, then rebuilt the design for Yaper’s design system. I worked end to end with the product manager and engineering.

Map the states

I used Claude to help organize the product proposal into a state matrix covering card and PIN entry, verification, rejection and payout. I worked with the product and engineering context to shape the states the design needed to support.

Prototype the interaction

I used Figma Make to create a rough, clickable version of the flow. This let me work through the sequence as a user would: select a brand, enter card details, wait for verification and follow the transaction status.

Prepare the design for handoff

Once the flow and states were mapped, I rebuilt the experience using Yaper’s design system and prepared the interaction details for engineering handoff.

04

Three decisions that shaped the experience

Show the payout in rupees

A discount rate makes the user do mental arithmetic during a high-stakes moment.

Instead of showing only “Sell at 4% discount,” the design shows the result directly: for a ₹10,000 card, the user receives ₹9,600 after a ₹400 platform fee.

Separate submission, verification and payment

“Your card has been submitted” can sound like the money is already on its way. The flow separates the transaction into clear stages: Submitted → Verified → Paid.

The user can see whether the card was received, whether it passed verification and whether the payout has been sent.

Explain what’s happening during verification

A generic loader during a balance check can look like a frozen app. The design uses a dedicated verification screen to explain what is being checked and show progress.

After verification, the UPI payout is instant. Verification and payout are shown as separate steps, so “instant payout” doesn’t imply the card check itself is instant.

05

Build the experience into Yaper

The early prototype helped me explore the interaction, but it wasn’t the final handoff design. I rebuilt the flow using Yaper’s design system so it would fit the wider app experience.

That included applying Yaper’s semantic color, typography and radius tokens; using core components; and defining input states for focus, errors and masked card details.

07

Make trust visible at each step

The flow uses a few patterns to make the transaction easier to understand:

  • Set expectations earlyShow the steps (select a brand, enter details and receive a UPI payout) before asking for the card number and PIN.

  • Show the amount clearlyDisplay the card value, platform fee and expected payout together.

  • Protect sensitive detailsMask card numbers and PINs after entry.

  • Keep a transaction recordShow previous submissions, payout status and rejection reasons in transaction history.

08

Give engineering the states, not just the screens

For handoff, I documented how the flow should behave across field and transaction states. The status sequence was:

SUBMITTED → CHECKING → VERIFIED → PAID or REJECTED

The handoff also covered field states such as typing, focus, loading, success and error, along with copy for validation messages and cases such as network timeouts, duplicate submissions and cards with no balance.

The design was handed off to engineering. The feature had not launched at the time of this case study, so these details describe the intended experience rather than a verified production behavior.

09

What I learned

This project reinforced the value of working through transaction logic before visual polish. Prototyping helped me shape the flow and its edge cases before handoff, while collaboration with product and engineering helped connect the screens to the underlying transaction states.

The key design question was not only “Can the user submit a card?” It was also “Can they tell what has happened to their card and when they’ll receive their money?”

What I’d measure after launch

Because the feature had not launched, I could not claim a measured user or business result. After launch, I’d track:

  • The percentage of users who start and complete a submission
  • Rejections by reason
  • Time from card submission to verification
  • Time from verification to UPI payout
  • Support contacts per transaction
  • Repeat submissions

These measures would help show whether the flow was understandable for users and whether the new fulfilment model delivered the projected business impact.
