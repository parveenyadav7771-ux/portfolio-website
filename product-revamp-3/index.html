All work Yaper Product Revamp 3.0 · Recently shipped

Making a growing product easier to understand

Yaper was working, but as it grew, the experience became harder to follow.

Role
Product Designer
Status
Recently shipped
Scope
UX strategy, research, information architecture, interaction design, UI, and design system
Focus
Discovery, orders, payments, account management, and notifications

Overview

Users contacted support to ask about payment status. Some created multiple Yaper accounts to receive payments into different bank accounts. Others needed help understanding what their wallet balance represented.

These signals pointed to a broader problem: Yaper’s structure was becoming harder to understand than the tasks people came to do.

I led a product revamp across discovery, orders, payments, account management, and notifications.

The goal: make the experience clearer by organizing it around how people explore Yaper, manage orders, and understand their money.

What users and support were telling us

Feedback from direct users and the support team revealed recurring friction:

  • 01Users asked support about the payment status of their orders.
  • 02Some users created multiple Yaper accounts to receive payments into different bank accounts.
  • 03Users needed help understanding what their wallet balance represented.
  • 04The sign-in flow asked people to commit before they could explore Yaper.

These weren’t isolated screen-level issues. They showed that people had to understand Yaper’s internal structure to answer practical questions about orders, earnings, and payments.

Research input: Direct user feedback and recurring themes from support conversations and tickets.

The design shift

Rather than redesigning screens one by one, I focused on making the product’s structure easier to understand. Three principles guided the work:

  1. 1Let people understand Yaper before asking them to sign in.
  2. 2Put information where people naturally look for it.
  3. 3Give a growing product clearer places for discovery, payments, and updates.
01

Build confidence before asking for commitment

Previously, people had to log in before exploring Yaper. That asked for personal information before giving them a reason to trust the product. I changed the sequence:

BeforeOpen Yaper → Log in → Explore
AfterOpen Yaper → Explore → Take an action → Log in when needed

Once people could explore without signing in, the home screen needed to do more than show deals. It needed to explain what Yaper was and show that the platform was active.

I brought activity and credibility signals into the experience, including real-time earnings activity and cumulative earnings and user counts.

I also added contextual guidance to the first-deal experience, so help could appear in the flow instead of waiting to be found.

Trade-off
Removing login from the entry point reduced early user identification and account-level personalization. Using the home screen to build trust also left less space for deals. Contextual guidance added information to the interface.
Why I accepted it
People needed a reason to trust Yaper before being asked to commit. For a first-time user, useful guidance during a deal mattered more than keeping every screen as minimal as possible.
What changed
Login became necessary when an action required an account, rather than a barrier to exploring the product.
02

Put payment information where people look for it

Yaper separated orders and transactions. But people weren’t looking for a transaction record by itself. They wanted to know:

“Did I get paid for this order?”

I brought payment details into the order context, including payment status, UTR, and receiving bank. Users could understand what happened without moving between separate sections.

The wallet created a related problem. Its balance could be read as money available to withdraw, while users needed to understand how much they had earned through Yaper.

Instead of making the wallet responsible for more information, I moved earnings closer to orders, where users were already looking for the outcome of their activity.

Trade-off
Order details became richer and could feel denser. Moving earnings out of the wallet also meant there was no single place for every money-related concept.
Why I accepted it
The information made more sense in context. An order should explain its payment, and earnings should relate to the activity that generated them.
What changed
Payment details were connected to orders, and earnings could be understood alongside those orders.
03

Support real payment needs with one account

Some users needed to receive payments into different bank accounts. With limited flexibility, one workaround was to create multiple Yaper accounts.

BeforeYaper account → Bank A, plus a second Yaper account → Bank B
AfterOne Yaper account → Bank A, Bank B, Bank C

I treated that workaround as a sign that the product wasn’t supporting the user’s actual need. The redesign allowed multiple bank accounts to be managed from one Yaper account.

Trade-off
Multiple accounts added configuration. Users needed to know which bank was selected and where each payment would go.
Why I accepted it
Managing more bank options was preferable to maintaining multiple Yaper identities.
What changed
Users could manage several payment destinations from one account.
04

Give the growing product clearer places to live

As Yaper grew, the home screen started to hold deals, orders, payment information, and notifications. It became harder to understand what deserved attention.

I gave notifications a dedicated place so updates did not have to compete with discovery.

I also introduced a lightweight preference layer based on bank and card information, without asking users to share sensitive card details.

Trade-off
A notification centre adds another destination. Asking for preferences adds an upfront step.
Why I accepted it
Separate responsibilities could reduce the burden of putting everything on the home screen. Keeping preferences lightweight created a starting point for more relevant deals and notifications without collecting card details.
What changed
Notifications had their own place, and Yaper had a foundation for future personalization.

What changed across Yaper

The redesign connected discovery, orders, payments, account management, and notifications around clearer user needs.

  • More room to explorePeople could open Yaper before signing in.

  • More context upfrontThe home experience surfaced activity and credibility signals.

  • Clearer payment detailsPayment information appeared with the order it explained.

  • Clearer earningsEarnings were connected to user activity instead of an ambiguous wallet balance.

  • More payment flexibilityMultiple bank accounts could be managed from one Yaper account.

  • Clearer focusNotifications no longer competed with discovery on the home screen.

What I learned

The biggest lesson wasn’t about screens. It was about mental models.

  • A separate Transactions tab made sense to the system.
  • One bank account made sense when Yaper was smaller.
  • Putting everything on the home screen made sense when there was less to manage.

As products grow, the assumptions behind them can stop matching how people use them. This revamp was about recognizing those gaps and deciding which trade-offs would make the product easier to understand.

Planned measures, not results

What we’ll track next

The redesign has recently shipped, so post-launch impact data isn’t available yet. The next step is to establish a baseline and track whether the changes address the issues raised by users and support.

  • Payment-status support tickets. Track volume and themes over time.
  • Multiple-account workarounds. Monitor reports of users creating extra accounts to manage bank destinations.
  • Order payment comprehension. Use research or usability sessions to see whether users can find and understand payment details.
  • Explore-to-sign-up behavior. Measure how people move from exploring to actions that require an account.
  • Notification engagement. Assess whether a dedicated notification centre helps people find and act on important updates.
